A world that learns what it has never seen

OpenWorld is a role-playing world whose state and rules are all code. Where the code runs out, a language model writes more — once, for everyone.

Ask a neural world model to pour water on a campfire and it will show you steam. Walk away, come back a minute later, and the campfire may not be there. Ask a hand-made game to do the same and the fire goes out every time, for every player, for years — unless nobody thought to write that it does, and then nothing happens at all.

OpenWorld is our attempt to have both: a role-playing world as exact and lasting as a game, that still answers when a player tries something nobody planned for. Its state and its rules are all code. Where the code runs out, a language model writes more, once, for everyone. This first post explains how, and why we think it scales.

01Two roads to an open world

An open world, in the sense we care about, is one where you can try something nobody planned and the world answers sensibly. There are two roads toward it today, and each fails where the other succeeds.

Learned world models — Genie, GameNGen, Oasis and their successors — generate the world frame by frame from your actions. They accept any input, so in that sense they are open. But the world lives only in pixels and a short context: about three seconds of history for GameNGen, sixteen frames for Genie, a minute or so of visual memory for Genie 3. Leave a room and come back, and the room may have changed. Every frame costs a forward pass of a large network, for every player.

Hand-built games are the opposite. Dwarf Fortress, the Elder Scrolls, the chemistry of Breath of the Wild: vast, persistent, exactly consistent, and nearly free to run. They are also closed. An interaction the designers did not foresee simply does not exist.

02Openness is sparse

Our starting point is that openness is sparse and structured. A player may try anything, but what players actually try falls into a heavy-tailed set of situations: a kind of thing, receiving a kind of event, at a particular point of its rules. Once a situation has an answer, every later instance of it is ordinary gameplay.

So openness is needed only at the frontier, the first time a situation is reached. Everything behind the frontier is best left to code: exact, persistent, composable, readable, and free to run again. That suggests a division of labour:

  • Code simulates everything, every frame: people keep their days, fire spreads over dry grass and rain puts it out, the story runs its course.
  • A model writes code only at the frontier, when the world meets a situation its rules leave open.
  • What it writes is kept in a library every player shares, so each situation is paid for once.
How it works: a rule answers; where none is written, the model is asked once; everyone after runs the rule.

03Rules belong to things

Every change in OpenWorld is the outcome of an event, and every outcome is decided by rules that belong to the thing the event happens to. A campfire's rules say what rain does to a campfire; a slime's rules say what fire does to a slime. A rule may read anything in the world but write only its own thing. To affect anything else it sends that thing an event, and the other thing answers for its own nature.

A rule of the base library: what fire does to whatever it reaches, written once for every kind with the same tags and statuses.

Rules address kinds — types, tags and statuses — never one particular bucket or one particular slime, so a rule written once holds for every instance. The events come from a small, closed set: a deliberate act, an impulse from another thing, contact, a bump, time passing, a threshold crossed, being seen, being stuck, being broken.

A rule is a tree of conditions with outcomes at its leaves, and some leaves are deliberately left open: the places where a thing's nature is not yet written. A fire slime that takes fire. An axe used on a winch. A rope held out to a wolf.

An open leaf: the base library says what oil does to most things, and leaves the rest unwritten.
The game never depends on the model. With no model connected, an open leaf falls back to its default — usually, nothing happens — and the game plays to its end like any other.

04One event, many consequences

Because everything is a thing with rules, nothing happens on its own. Lightning sets a dead tree alight. Rain puts out a campfire. Someone takes advantage of the storm to steal, and a bounty goes up. Every event reaches the people, places and things around it, and each of those reaches more.

One stormy day at the Ford, and everything it touches: places, people, kinds of thing, rules and quests.

This is also why the model's answer has to be a rule and not a picture. A rule composes with the physics already there: once a ferrykeeper knows to wait by the fire when she is stuck in the rain, the fire, the rain and the people around her all go on working as before.

05When the rules run out

When an event reaches an open leaf, the engine holds the thing it happened to — only that thing; the rest of the world keeps running — and sends the modder a request: the event, the path it took through the rule's tree, the participants and what surrounds them, and a specification of everything a rule may say.

The modder first looks for an answer in what the world already has: the request's own types, then the catalogue of existing kinds. Only if nothing fits does it create a new kind, with a small picture of its own. Its answer is a rule for that type, placed at that open leaf.

A learned rule: what pouring oil does, written once for every liquid of its kind.

The engine treats the answer as untrusted code. It checks the rule's schema, its references to the ontology, and that it writes only its own thing; it replays the triggering event in a sandbox; and an adversarial auditor plays against the rule, looking for farms, softlocks and runaway chains. A finding counts only if the sandbox reproduces it with the rule and not without it. Only then is the rule added to the library. A rule can only fill an open leaf, so the model can never change what the authors wrote.

An answer takes about ten seconds and costs about five cents, and it is paid for once: the library is shared, so the next player to meet the same situation runs it as plain code. Players choose how much of this they want, for each kind of work, from off to high.

The AI setting: how much the model does, for each kind of work. The wait is the same at every level.

06A thousand maps, and what comes next

Because the per-frame loop holds no model at all, the world can be as large as its content allows. OpenWorld has 1,024 maps — 59 made by hand, 965 across six regions round the Holy City — 1,654 people in 780 households, each with a look and a day of their own, and 40,562 items of 1,013 kinds. It runs in a browser tab. Maps far behind the player sleep, and catch up when you come back.

The world in numbers: one map, then a thousand; one person, then a crowd; a chest that will not stop pouring.

The world itself remembers the Long Night, when the sun went out until the saint Astra bore five holy torments and it rose again. Now the days are growing short once more, and a pilgrim comes down from the mountain with a fairy at their side.

There is a second thread we will write about soon: the modder improving itself — keeping a memory, writing its own skills, revising its own working loop — as the world it works on grows. We will post every two weeks: how the rules are built, how the modder answers, what players try first, and what the world learns from them.
