A world that learns as it goes

OpenWorld is a role-playing world, and its state and rules are code. The interesting part is what happens when play needs a rule or an item nobody wrote: a language model can make it, and then everyone shares it.

Anyone who has played a sandbox game knows the frustration: every interaction in it was written by the authors in advance. Pour water on a campfire and it goes out, because the authors wrote a rule for “water puts out fire.” But have a sudden idea, say tipping a pot of hot soup over a snowman, and the game does not respond… Programmers call this undefined behavior (UB), and a traditional game usually handles it by doing nothing at all.

Making new things is just as limited. You might want more dishes from the kitchen, a few wines with their own flavour, or tools and armour of your own design, but if the authors never defined those items, they cannot happen.

What OpenWorld wants is a world that keeps running steadily while it encourages players to think of anything and try it, with AI in the background taking care of whatever you need! When an interaction is undefined, the AI fills it in on the spot; when an item is undefined, the AI makes it. And each new rule or item is written only once, and every player after that simply uses it.

This blog is about how that works, and what we want to do next.

01The two roads we already had

An open world, for us, means you can do something nobody planned, and the world gives you an answer that holds together. There are two ways people have been going after that. Each one is strong exactly where the other is weak.

One way is learned world models: Genie, GameNGen, Oasis, and what came after them. They generate the picture frame by frame from what you do, and they will take any input, so in that sense they are open. The world only lives in pixels and a short window, though. GameNGen remembers about three seconds. Genie keeps sixteen frames. Genie 3’s visual memory tops out around a minute. Stay with something for longer than that and you come back to a place that has already changed. It can also draw something new out of nowhere, but not long after, that thing may simply be gone. Add the cost of running the model, and it is hard to see this as a game people pay to play. You get demos and papers.

The other way is the traditional game, which is what studios have been making all along. Dwarf Fortress, the Elder Scrolls, the simulation in Zelda: Breath of the Wild: a lot of space, state that sticks, cause and effect that stay consistent, and almost nothing to run. Closed, though. An interaction nobody wrote into the rules does not happen; an item nobody planned for does not appear.

02We think openness is a sparse problem

The starting point is simple. Open does not mean generating everything as you go. What a player can try looks infinite, but the interactions that actually come up, the interesting ones, sit in a long tail. Solve a situation once and the next time it should just run. The model can also look at what people keep doing and improve along that trail. In a sense that is recursive.

So you only need the open part the first time a situation comes up. After that, code is the better place for it: exact, persistent, something you can compose, something you can read, and free to run again.

A few working ideas follow from that:

Code still simulates everything, every frame. People go about their day. Fire runs along dry grass and rain puts it out. The story stays on its track.

The model writes code only when it has to, when the world hits a gap in the rules. And what it writes stays. It goes into a rule library every player shares, so a special situation is paid for once. New items work the same way: the model makes a new kind only when nothing in the world fits.

How it works: if a rule exists, the rule answers. If it does not, we ask the model once. After that, everyone just runs the rule.

For new items, we keep a kind of NPC in the game: makers, like Garrick the smith. You can chat with one and ask whether something new could be made, say “hollow a log into a bowl for stew.” He answers in a few seconds with a recipe, and the picture follows; if it is something you can already make, he tells you how.

Making a wooden bowl with Garrick the smith: the idea, the checks, made, then put to use.

03A rule is what you get when things interact

In this world, change comes from events. What an event does is decided by the rules of the thing it happens to. A campfire has its own rules for what rain does. A slime has its own rules for what fire does. A rule can read anything in the world, but it can only change the thing it belongs to. To affect something else, it sends that thing an event, and the other thing answers with its own rules.

From the base library: what happens when fire touches something. Same tag, same status, same kind of thing: write it once.

Rules should be about the groups we already use (types, tags, and statuses), not one particular object. Not one special slime, and not a bed that only exists in one scene. Write the rule and every thing of that kind gets it. The events themselves come from a fixed, limited set: the player doing something, a hit from outside, contact, a bump, time passing, a number crossing a line, coming into view, getting stuck, taking damage.

So a rule is basically a tree of conditions, and the result usually sits on a leaf. Some leaves we leave blank on purpose. We have not said yet what this thing does when that condition hits. A fire slime that gets burned. A player who decides an axe should pry a winch. Someone trying to hand a rope to a wolf. That is a lot of room we did not have to fill in ahead of time.

A blank leaf. The library says what oil does to most things. The rest is left open.
So the game itself does not depend on a large model. With no model connected, a blank leaf takes the default, which is usually nothing. You can still play it through, the same as any other game.

04We might be looking at Doctor Strange’s 14 million futures

Everything has a nature of its own, so nothing happens on its own. Lightning hits dead wood and it burns. Rain falls and the campfire goes out. Someone steals under cover of the storm, and a bounty goes up around the Ford. A new thing is no different. The first time a freshly made wooden bowl is knocked on an anvil, the world gains a rule for it, and from then on it is caught up in these chains too. Each thing that happens stirs the people and objects nearby, and that stirs more. The futures do not all come out the same. That is the appeal of an open world.

A storm at the Ford, and what it sets off: the place, the people passing through, the objects, the rules already there, and the quests that come out of it.

A rule can sit in the logic that is already running. Once the ferry keeper learns to stay by the fire when she is stuck in the rain, the fire, the rain, and everything around her keep going as they did.

05When we reach the far side

When an event lands on a blank branch, we do not want the engine to freeze the whole world. We pause only the thing that was hit. Everything else keeps moving.

The model looks first at natures the world already has: the types on the event, then the catalogue of creatures and objects. If nothing fits, it adds a new kind, and a small picture of it. What comes back is a rule for that type, written into the blank.

A rule it learned: what happens when you pour a liquid. One definition covers that kind of fluid.

The model can propose new rules and create new items, but the engine does not take them as they come: every change passes three checks in turn. The first is static: is the format valid, do the things it names exist, and does a rule stay within the thing it belongs to. The second is the economy check, there to stop resource farming. It writes every conversion in the world as a linear programme, and if any combination can make something from nothing, the patch is refused before it ever goes live. The third is a sandbox audit, which looks for safety problems such as the game getting stuck or chains running out of control. Some rules we wrote as standing orders, and the engine cannot change those either. Open does not mean anything goes. Freedom is not a blank check.

Right now a request is about ten seconds and about five cents, and you do not pay that forever. The library is shared. The next player who hits the same situation just runs it as ordinary code. Players can also turn the model up or down by kind of interaction, from off to a lot.

The in-game AI panel. You set how much the model joins in, by kind of interaction, from off to high. The wait is the same on every setting.

06A thousand maps, and what comes next

Because the loop that runs every frame has no model in it, the world can be as big as the content.

OpenWorld currently has:

from towns and villages to the rooms behind their doors
1,024maps
in 780 households, each with their own look and their own day
1,654people
of 1,013 kinds, and growing as players and the AI make new ones
40,562items

Scenes the player has left do not have to keep running at full cost. When they come back, we write down what changed in the meantime. Cheap, and it works.

The size of the world: one map, then a thousand; one person, then a crowd; a chest that does not run out.

There is something else we will write about later. The modder keeps improving while the world it works on gets bigger: it keeps a memory, writes skills for itself, and revises how it works.

We expect a post about every two weeks: how the rules get built, how the modder answers, what players try first, and what the world picks up from them.
