When nobody wrote the rule

OpenWorld is a pixel-art RPG in which everything, the weather and the people included, runs on rules. Where a rule is missing, a language model writes one, and the game keeps it.

Ask a neural world model to pour water on a campfire and it will show you steam. Walk away for a minute and come back, and the campfire may be gone. Ask a traditional game and the fire goes out every time, for every player, for years. Unless nobody wrote that it does; then nothing happens at all.

We want both at once: a world as exact and lasting as a game, which still answers when a player does something nobody planned for. In OpenWorld the state and the rules are all code. Where the code has nothing to say, a language model writes the missing rule, and the game keeps it for every player after.

01Two roads to an open world

By an open world we mean one where you can do something nobody planned and get a sensible answer. Two kinds of system come close today.

Learned world models, such as Genie, GameNGen and Oasis, draw each frame from the frames before it and your input. You can ask them anything. But the world lives only in pixels and a short context: about three seconds for GameNGen, sixteen frames for Genie, around a minute of visual memory for Genie 3. A room you leave may be different when you come back, and every frame costs a pass through a large network, for every player.

Traditional games are the other way round. Dwarf Fortress, the Elder Scrolls games and the chemistry of Breath of the Wild are vast and consistent, remember everything and cost almost nothing to run. But an interaction their designers never wrote does not exist in them.

02Openness is sparse

What makes a mix of the two possible is that openness is sparse. A player may try anything, but what players actually try gathers into situations: one kind of thing, meeting one kind of event, at one point in its rules. A few situations come up all the time; most come up rarely. Once a situation has an answer, every later case of it is ordinary play.

So a model is needed only the first time a situation comes up. Everything else is better left to code, which is exact, keeps what it is told, and costs nothing to run again. In OpenWorld, code runs the world every frame: people go about their day, fire spreads over dry grass and rain puts it out, the story moves on. A model is called only when the world meets a situation its rules leave open, and what it writes goes into a library every player shares, so each situation is paid for once.

The campfire has a rule and runs it. The stranded ferrykeeper has none, so the model is asked once; after that, everyone runs its answer as a rule.

03Rules belong to things

What rain does to a campfire is written in the campfire's rules. What fire does to a slime is written in the slime's. Every change in OpenWorld is the outcome of an event, and the outcome is decided by the rules of the thing the event happened to. A rule may look at anything in the world, but it may change only its own thing. To affect something else, it sends that thing an event, and that thing's own rules decide what happens.

A rule from the base library: what fire does to anything it reaches. It is written for tags and statuses, so it covers every kind that has them.

Rules are written for kinds of thing, by type, tag and status, never for one bucket or one slime, so a rule written once holds for every bucket in the world. Events come from a short, fixed list: something done on purpose, an impulse from another thing, touching, bumping, time passing, a threshold crossed, being seen, being stuck, being broken.

A rule is a tree of conditions with outcomes at its leaves, and some leaves are left open on purpose, where nobody has yet written what a thing does. What happens when an axe is used on a winch? When a rope is held out to a wolf?

An open leaf: the base library says what oil does to most things and leaves the rest unwritten.
The game never needs the model. With no model connected, an open leaf falls back to its default, which is usually that nothing happens, and the game plays through to its end like any other.

04One storm at the Ford

Because everything has rules, one event runs on into the next. On one stormy day at the Ford, lightning sets the dead tree alight and rain soaks Hesper's campfire. Vigo takes Marta's bread in the confusion, Holt sees him, and a bounty goes up. Each step is one thing's rules sending an event to the next.

One stormy day at the Ford, and everything it reaches: places, people, kinds of thing, rules and quests.

This is also why the model answers with a rule and not with frames. A rule fits in with the ones already there: once the ferrykeeper knows to wait by the fire when she is stuck in the rain, the rain still falls, the fire still burns down, and the people round her still go about their day.

05When there is no rule

When an event reaches an open leaf, the engine pauses the one thing it happened to, and the rest of the world keeps going. It sends the modder a request: the event, the path it took through the rule's tree, who was involved and what was around them, and a specification of everything a rule is allowed to say.

The modder looks first at what the world already has: the types in the request, then the catalogue of existing kinds. Only when nothing fits does it make a new kind, with a small picture of its own. Its answer is a rule for that type, placed at that open leaf.

A learned rule: what pouring oil does, written once for every liquid of its kind.

The engine treats the answer as untrusted code. It checks the rule's format, its references to the ontology, and that it changes only its own thing. It replays the triggering event in a sandbox. Then an auditor plays against the rule, hunting for item farms, softlocks and chains that never stop; a problem counts only if the sandbox shows it with the rule and not without it. A rule that passes all of this goes into the library. Rules can only fill open leaves, so the model can never change anything the authors wrote.

An answer takes about ten seconds and costs about five cents, and it is paid for once: the library is shared, so the next player who meets the same situation just runs the rule. Players choose how much of this they want for each kind of work, from off to high.

The AI setting in the game's options: for each kind of work, how much the model does, from off to high. The wait is the same at every level.

06A thousand maps in a browser tab

No model runs in the per-frame loop, so the world can be as big as its content. OpenWorld has 1,024 maps, from towns and villages to the rooms behind their doors. It has 1,654 people in 780 households, each with a face and a day of their own, and 40,562 items of 1,013 kinds. It all runs in a browser tab. Maps you leave far behind go to sleep, and catch up when you come back.

The world in numbers: one map, then a thousand; one person, then a crowd; a chest that will not stop pouring.
