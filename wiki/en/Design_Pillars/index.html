Design Pillars

This page records the principles OpenWorld's design follows. Each one notes the decision it comes from. For how a particular system works, see that system's page.

The world is code, and it learns what it has never seen

The game's state and rules are code, and the game is a complete, ordinary game in itself: it can be played from start to finish without AI. When you do something no rule defines, the world asks an AI, writes the answer down as a rule and keeps it, and from then on every player's world responds that way. See The World Learns.

  • There is only one AI interface: the outcome of an event. If it is defined, the rule applies; if not, the AI is asked; with no AI, an error or a time-out, the default outcome of that place applies (usually nothing happens).
  • A rule writes only itself. A rule belongs to one class of things (its “host”); it can read anything but change only itself. To affect something else, it sends that thing an event, and the thing answers by itself. Lava's rule is “send scorching to whatever is on top”; a person's rule is “lose health when scorched”; what a fire slime does when scorched is up to its own rule, and if that is undefined, the AI is asked.
  • Patches only fill gaps; they never change behavior that is already defined. A learned rule belongs to one kind of thing only and fills only the place it was asked about; what holds for a whole class of things is written into the game's own rules. The same question is asked only once.
  • Taking and putting are physics and never go to the AI; using one thing on another is where the AI answers. See Controls.
  • Design as if there were AI even when there is none. New behavior goes through events and rules, never around them in the engine; a deliberate act no rule covers is left as a gap, for the AI.

Source: the event model settled on 2026-10-02; the discussion of the five inputs on 2026-10-05; the decision of 2026-10-09 that a learned rule takes only one place (docs/design.md §5.16).

Goals are states, not paths

Each step's goal is written as “what the world should become”: standing on the far bank, the bridge passable, the knight fallen, the Crown of Thorns on you. Any means the world allows counts, including means the world has learned from players. The Ford teaches you three ways to cross the river (repair the winch, fell the dead tree into a bridge, freeze the river), and any other way counts as long as you get across.

Source: the design of the Ford (2026-10-02); carried on in Chapter 1.

Places teach, not text

The way to do something is first shown by the place itself: a frost slime guards the spot where the riverbank freezes, a dead tree stands alone at a narrow point, a broken rope hangs from the winch. People talk about their own lives, not about controls. The tutorial teaches one action per lesson, and the keys it has not taught are locked (see Tutorial).

The world exists before you

People have their own days, relationships and things left unsaid (see Residents). They live by their schedules and notice what you do to their things. The world also changes on its own: storms at dusk, monsters at night, days growing shorter one by one (see Day and Night).

General systems, no special cases

  • No judgment hinges on “is it the player”. Who attacks whom depends on factions and temper; who sees what depends on witnesses; who collapses instead of dying depends on the “essential” tag; using something on yourself depends on the “self” target. These are all data any actor can carry. Only the input knows which actor the keyboard controls. Source: 2026-10-02.
  • One concept has one mechanism. Everything has durability and leaves the materials it is made of when broken; anything can change in stages over time (crops growing, stumps growing back into trees, hens laying eggs); people, monsters, companions and bosses use the same set of behavior goals; the story has one script. See Durability and Breaking. Source: the world model of 2026-10-04.
  • Numbers are parameters of the world, not hard-coded in the engine. See Special:Values.

Legible

What each input will do is shown before you press it, on the prompt bar above your character's head, with a frame drawn round the target cell; a way of doing things the world has never seen is marked “?”. Everything the world learns is recorded in the Threadbook, along with who taught it.

Learn craft, do not copy content

What we learn from other games is craft: letting the place teach, giving people lives, a warm interface, good game feel. We do not copy other games' stories, gameplay loops or systems. The former “Hearthvale” prototype copied Stardew's shipping bin, friendship points, gifts, festivals, seasons and stamina; it was deleted entirely on 2026-10-02. That is why this game has no energy, seasons, festivals or friendship. Basic gameplay such as an inventory, crafting, levels and satiety is welcome.

Before designing any mechanism, we first look at how other games do it (Elin above all), then adapt it to this game's principles; we design from scratch only where no one else has done it.

Small and polished first, then larger

The target scale is hundreds of maps, thousands of people and tens of thousands of items. The present world (the Ford and the maps that grew around it) is a development scaffold: make it small and polished first, then grow it. Small is no reason to refuse growth: new maps, people and systems are welcome as long as they are general and of good quality. Source: 2026-09-30, 2026-10-02.

Completeness must be proven

“Complete” does not mean giving a few examples; it means a proof:

  • Completeness of the inputs: every key × every held thing × every target × every layer has a definite behavior or an explicit gap (2026-10-02).
  • Every thing in the development world must correspond to a requirement, checked by a test on the real map (tools/test/demo.test.ts).
  • The wiki likewise: every thing and every system in the game must have a page; see Special:Completeness.
