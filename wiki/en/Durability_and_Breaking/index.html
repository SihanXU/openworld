Durability and Breaking

From the OpenWorld Wiki · Gameplay

Everything in the world that can be broken has durability: a tree, a rock, a wall, a chest; a creature's durability is its health. How much durability a blow knocks off depends on what is struck and what it is struck with. When durability reaches 0, the thing breaks: a tree falls, a rock splits, a chest comes apart into planks, a creature falls.

The ground itself cannot be broken: the terrain laid at the very bottom, such as grass, dirt and water, has no durability.

How much one blow knocks off

Striking with something (the left button, or Space to strike ahead) is called a “blow”. A blow uses the weapon or tool in your hand; with empty hands (and likewise when you strike with Space while holding something else) it uses the weapon in your main hand, and failing that your own body: a punch. Pressing the left button with anything else in hand is not a blow; it uses that thing on yourself.

  1. Force = your own attack + the power of what is in your hand. The player starts with attack 2; a stone axe has power 5, so one chop is 7.
  2. Too hard? If the material of what is in your hand is softer than the target, nothing comes off, and you see only “Too hard.”.
  3. Efficiency: force is multiplied by efficiency. Efficiency depends on what use the thing in your hand has (axe, pick, shovel, hammer) or what edge (cut, blunt, pierce, dig, magic), against what material the target is. When a thing has several uses and edges, the best one counts.
  4. Defense: the target's defense is subtracted, with at least 1 knocked off. Creatures also have dodging, blocking and so on; see Combat.

  • A “cut” edge, like swords and knives, is 0.5 against wood and 0.1 against stone.
  • A “blunt” edge, like fists and clubs, is 0.25 against wood, 0.5 against plants and 0.1 against stone.
  • Against flesh (the bodies of creatures): cut, blunt, pierce and magic edges are all 1; only “dig” is 0.25.

Too hard

Every material has a hardness. If the material of what is in your hand is less hard than the target, you cannot touch it.

  • The body is flesh, hardness 1. Fists can break wood (1) and plants (0) but not stone or ore (2). So bare hands can take down trees and bushes, but cannot break stone.
  • Wooden things (wooden club, wooden bow) cannot touch stone either.
  • Stone tools (stone axe, stone pickaxe) have hardness 2 and can break both stone and ore.

The hardness of each material is in Substances.

When it breaks

When durability reaches 0, it depends on whether the thing has a rule of its own:

  1. A rule of its own: a tree falls and becomes a stump, dropping 2 logs and 1 stick (see Woodcutting); those who “cannot die”, like the residents and Lumi, go down for a while and get up again; a boss leaves its reward when it falls (see Bosses); a training dummy falls over and stands up again.
  2. No rule of its own, but it says what it is made of: it comes apart into what it was made of, with only half of each coming back (a salvage rate of 0.5, with a chance of one more for a fraction); what it carried or held all falls out.
  3. Neither: what happens when it breaks is not written in the rules; it is something the world has not seen. With no AI connected, it simply disappears and what was in it falls out.

Materials that come apart pop out; walk close and they fly into your bag. What a fallen creature leaves does not pop; it lies quietly where it fell, and you press E to pick it up.

The walls of houses

A house on a map is not a picture pasted on the ground: under the picture every cell is a real wall, with durability, a material and an owner. Striking it leaves marks, but it does not give way: the only way into a house is its door.

The house is drawn as one picture cut into a piece per cell, and each piece follows the wall beneath it:

  • Cracks: when the wall loses durability, its piece of the picture cracks: one crack at a third gone, wider at two thirds, and at the deepest a corner falls out.
  • It holds: struck down to its last point of durability, the wall stops there: a little dust rises, “The wall holds.” It still blocks the way, drops nothing, and the picture shows no hole, only cracks.
  • Burnt black: fire does not burn through either: when it goes out, a wooden, canvas or thatch wall is a burnt wall, and its piece of the picture darkens.

The houses in towns, chapels, keeps, ruins, the fences on the maps and the walls round a room inside are all walls like this. Cave walls and cliffs are not: a pickaxe cuts through them (see Mining).

Which tool bites deepest depends on the wall's material: an axe for wooden walls, a pickaxe for stone (an axe on stone has an efficiency of 0.25 and barely marks it), canvas and thatch take a gash at every blow, snow blocks a pickaxe.

Hacking at someone else's wall is a crime. The walls round a house belong to whoever owns the rooms its door leads into. Seen doing it, it is vandalism: the household, any passer-by who saw and the guards remember it, and the owner cries out and goes to the watch (a wall cannot be handed back, so they do not come to ask for it); gossip tells of it as “you smashed at so-and-so's wooden wall”. Nobody minds a wall of an empty house or one in the wild.

Walls you build yourself are different: a Wall put up with the build palette, on the Held Ground or anywhere, cracks as it loses durability and, struck to 0 with a weapon, breaks into planks; struck with an axe, pickaxe or hammer it comes loose whole (see Building). What you build, you can take down.

Why only half comes back

However many materials something takes to make, breaking it gives back at most half. That way no loop of “make it, then take it apart” can conjure materials out of nothing. To get a wall, floor, bed or workbench back as it was, knock it with an axe, pickaxe or hammer (left click) and it comes loose whole where it stood; press E to pick it up (close by, it is drawn into your bag by itself), rather than smashing it with a weapon.

Axe marks heal

A tree chopped once with an axe carries an axe mark (notched). If it is not chopped again within 10 seconds, the tree heals and its durability is full again. Durability knocked off anything else does not grow back by itself.

Fire burns durability too

A burning thing (burning) has an even chance every half second of losing 1 durability, with defense not counting. When it burns out: ground becomes ash, a tree becomes a charred tree, a wall on a map (wooden, canvas or thatch) becomes a burnt wall (it does not burn through), furniture and items become Ash. Structures such as Wall, Floor, Stairs and Door are still there when the fire goes out, only with less durability; if their durability runs out while they burn, they become a heap of ash and do not come apart into planks. See Elements.

Falling

Falling hurts too, as if you had struck yourself:

  • Falling 1 storey does no harm.
  • Every storey beyond that costs 6 health, with defense not counting.
  • Walking off the edge of an upper floor, the floor under your feet falling in, and walking off a cliff all count as falling.
  • Things that fly do not fall.

Storeys are in Storeys; the height of terrain is in Terrain.

Tips

  • Bare hands against stone only get “Too hard”. Make a stone pickaxe first.
  • Swords chop trees only half as fast as an axe; to chop trees, take an axe.
  • Hoes and shovels have only 0.25 efficiency against creatures; switch to a weapon for a fight.

  • 2026-10-21: The controls settled into four inputs: using a held thing on something moved to the right click (the left click is the held thing's own use: a tool or weapon swings, anything else is used on yourself), and picking up loose things moved to E (see Controls).
